Baltic Energy Transit Risk

Exposure of post-2022 Baltic energy import infrastructure

In brief

Russian pipeline gas into the eight Baltic littoral states collapsed from roughly 70 bcm in 2021 to almost nothing by 2023. In its place the region now leans on seaborne LNG, landed at a handful of coastal terminals that together hold about a third of the capacity which stopped arriving.

The problem is not the size of that gap. Lower demand and pipeline gas from the west and north have largely closed it. It is that what replaced the pipelines is concentrated in a few fixed points, sits closest to Russian territory precisely where it is smallest, and is one asset deep in every country that has it. Remove any single country’s largest terminal and its seaborne cover disappears.

This project maps that infrastructure and scores how exposed each terminal is, using distance to Russian territory, share of regional capacity, and the length of the supply route past the Danish Straits. The infrastructure has already been struck three times since 2022, so none of this is hypothetical.

Terminal capacities are current to 2024 and 2025; gas import dependency is Eurostat data for 2023. Every source is listed on the Data page.

The question

Before 2022, much of the Baltic region’s gas arrived by pipeline from Russia. After the invasion of Ukraine and the sabotage of the Nord Stream pipelines, that supply was cut or abandoned, and imports moved onto a small set of coastal terminals for liquefied natural gas (LNG), such as Klaipėda, Świnoujście, and Inkoo. These are the sites where tankers unload gas that has been chilled to a liquid for shipping, and turn it back into gas for the pipeline network.

That shift did not simply change where the gas comes from. It concentrated the supply of eight countries onto a handful of fixed coastal sites, each of which can be located on a map and counted. This project asks how concentrated, and how exposed:

Findings

  1. Concentration. Capacity is lopsided. Two southern terminals, Świnoujście in Poland and Mukran in Germany, hold about three fifths of the region’s operating import capacity, while the northeast leans on one or two terminals each: Inkoo for the Gulf of Finland and Klaipėda for Lithuania. The replacement for a continental pipeline network is, in practice, five working coastal sites, with a sixth planned at Gdańsk.
  2. Proximity. The terminals closest to Russian territory are Hamina, at the eastern end of the Gulf of Finland, and Klaipėda and Gdańsk, which sit beside the Kaliningrad exclave. Proximity and capacity do not always line up: the two largest terminals, Świnoujście and Mukran, are among the farthest from Russia, which is what the exposure score is built to show.
  3. Transit through the Straits. Every LNG cargo bound for these terminals passes the Danish Straits. The Kiel Canal is the only other way in, and it is far too small for LNG carriers, so the Straits are the effective entrance. The terminals deepest inside the sea, in the Gulf of Finland, run the longest exposed route to reach port.
  4. The small states overbuilt on purpose. Set against what each country actually burns, Finland and Lithuania hold terminal capacity worth several times their own demand, while Germany and Poland cover only part of theirs by sea. The northeast did not build to meet its own consumption. It built a buffer, and its neighbours draw on it. That is a deliberate strategic choice, and it explains why the region’s most exposed assets are also its most useful ones.

The exposure score

Each terminal gets a score built from three things, each with a stated weight:

  • how close it is to Russian territory (weight 0.4),
  • how much of the region’s import capacity it holds (0.3),
  • how long its supply route past the Danish Straits runs (0.3).

Proximity gets the most weight because the question is about exposure to pressure from one actor, and distance is the most direct measure the public data supports. Capacity matters because a larger terminal does more damage if it goes down, and transit matters because a terminal deep inside the Baltic runs a longer voyage to be resupplied.

The weights are a judgement, not a finding. They sit in plain view on the Analysis page, changing them changes the ranking, and the sensitivity table there shows by how much. There is no hidden model. It is a starting point, not a final score.

Disruption to the infrastructure since 2022

This exposure is not hypothetical. Baltic energy and data links have been damaged on several occasions since 2022, mostly by vessels dragging anchors across the seabed.

Date Asset What happened
Sep 2022 Nord Stream 1 and 2 Subsea gas pipelines ruptured by underwater explosions off Bornholm.
Oct 2023 Balticconnector The Finland-Estonia gas pipeline and a telecoms cable were damaged by a ship’s anchor. The pipeline was shut for six months and returned to commercial use on 22 April 2024.
Dec 2024 Estlink 2 The Estonia-Finland power cable and four telecoms cables were cut on Christmas Day; a shadow-fleet tanker is the prime suspect. Repairs ran into 2025 and the link returned to commercial use on 20 June 2025.

These incidents are context, not events modelled here, but they are why the geography in this project is worth looking at closely. The Analysis page maps the main corridors and which have been hit.

What follows for policy

Four points follow from the maps rather than from theory. Each is a consequence of where the infrastructure physically sits:

  1. The north-east has no spare door. Borrowing the logic of the EU’s N-1 standard and removing each country’s largest terminal, the cover vanishes. Finland is left with Hamina, covering about a fifth of demand; Lithuania, Poland and Germany are left with no seaborne capacity at all. Backup then arrives by pipeline from neighbours, over exactly the links that have been damaged since 2022. Because Inkoo and Klaipėda also carry the spare capacity the region leans on, an outage there is not only a national problem.
  2. The connectors matter as much as the terminals. Estonia, Latvia and Sweden import through neighbours rather than terminals of their own, so their supply runs on pipes and cables like the Balticconnector. The incidents above hit precisely that layer.
  3. The Danish Straits are a shared dependency. Every cargo for all eight countries passes one entrance, so monitoring and patrol arrangements there, of the kind NATO stood up after the December 2024 cable cuts, protect the whole region at once.
  4. Floating terminals cut both ways. FSRUs were quick to add and can be moved to where they are needed, but they are few, chartered, and easy to count. Much of the region’s flexibility rests on a handful of ships.

Limitations

This measures geographic exposure, not the probability of disruption. A terminal close to a maritime boundary is not a prediction that anything will happen to it. It is a statement about geography.

A few specific caveats:

  • The score uses proxy measures (distance, capacity share) rather than a causal model. Different reasonable weights would shift the rankings.
  • It is a single snapshot. Capacity, routes, and terminals change.
  • The terminal capacities are compiled from operator reporting and Global Energy Monitor, current to 2024 and 2025. The dependence figures are Eurostat data for
    1. See the Data page.
  • Undersea cable and pipeline incidents in the region between 2023 and 2025 are context for the question, not events modelled here.

This shows where the exposure sits. It is not a risk forecast.

Terms used here

  • LNG (liquefied natural gas): natural gas chilled to a liquid so it can be carried by ship rather than a pipeline.
  • LNG terminal: the coastal site where those ships unload and the cargo is turned back into gas for the pipeline network. Some are built on land; some are a moored ship, known as an FSRU (a floating storage and regasification unit).
  • Capacity (bcm): how much gas a terminal can bring in over a year, measured in billion cubic metres.
  • Import dependency: the share of a country’s gas that comes from abroad rather than from its own production.
